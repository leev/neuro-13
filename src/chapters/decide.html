The chatbot in {{ch:chatbots}} writes. But a huge amount of software doesn't want an essay. It wants a decision: is this email spam? Which team should get this support ticket? How urgent is this message, from 0 to 1? Asking a chatbot those questions is like asking a novelist to tick a box. You get a paragraph back, and then your program has to dig the answer out of it.

In 2026 a new kind of model showed up built for exactly this job. The companies making them call them System One models. The name is borrowed from the psychologist Daniel Kahneman, who described two ways people think: System 1 is the fast gut reaction ("that's a dog"), System 2 is the slow, careful working-out ("what's 17 × 24?"). The first two well-known ones are Jev, from a company called TypeSafe AI (September 2026), and Clef, from Cloudflare (October 2026).

You don't chat with a System One model. You hand it some text plus a list of typed questions, and it hands back numbers. There are three kinds of question:

Question typeExampleWhat comes back
yes / noIs this spam?{"spam": 0.88}
choiceWhich topic: world, sports, business or scitech?{"choice": "sports", "confidence": 0.97}
scoreHow urgent is this, 0 to 1?{"urgency": 0.31}

Compare that with how {{ch:chatbots}} described a chatbot:

  • No writing loop. A chatbot runs its whole network once per token, over and over, until the story is finished. A decision model runs once and it's done. That's most of why it is so much faster and cheaper.
  • It can't ramble. Every possible answer is listed in the question. The model can be wrong, but it can't wander off and invent something that wasn't on the list.
  • The doubt is part of the answer. You don't get "spam". You get "88% spam", and your program decides what to do with an 88.
  • It can't do anything else. No stories, no code, no explanations. It is a box-ticker, and proud of it.

Let's build one

Here's the good news: you already know every part. A decision model is {{ch:multi}}'s snack picker, fed by {{ch:tokens}}'s lookup tables. We'll train one on 10,000 real news headlines stored in a plain file called headlines.csv — one news story per row (its headline plus the first sentence or two), each labelled with one of four topics (world, sports, business, scitech). The headlines come from a famous research collection called AG News, gathered in 2004.

The whole pipeline, start to finish:

  1. Chop the headline into words and look each one up in a dictionary to get its id number. The dictionary is built from the training headlines: any word seen at least 3 times gets an id.
  2. Each id grabs its row of the embedding table — 16 learned numbers per word.
  3. Average the rows into a single list of 16 numbers. That's the headline's "fingerprint".
  4. One layer turns 16 numbers into 4 scores, one per topic. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Done.

So is a token "just a dictionary word turned into a number"? In this model, yes, exactly — and you're about to see why real models don't do it that way. Our dictionary has about 8,750 words. Type a word that isn't in it and the model simply can't see it. That's the problem {{ch:tokens}}'s word-pieces solve: with pieces, nothing is ever missing.

The other thing to hold on to: the id number itself means nothing. Word 3,365 isn't "bigger" than word 483. The id is only a row number. All the meaning lives in the 16 numbers in that row — and those start out random and get nudged into place by the same training you've used since {{ch:training}}.

What just happened

Watch the two lines on the chart again. At the start the model is barely better than 25% — pure guessing, one in four. Then the embedding rows start to carry meaning and both lines take off. By the end it gets roughly 82 out of 100 unseen headlines right, using about 140,000 weights. Clef has 27 billion. Same idea, about two hundred thousand times bigger.

Three things worth poking at:

  • The grey pills. Words the dictionary has never seen are invisible to the model. Try a headline full of modern words — TikTok, ChatGPT, Fortnite. The training headlines are from 2004, so the model has never heard of any of them. A model only knows the world its data showed it.
  • "My dog ate my homework." None of the four topics fits. Sometimes the model shrugs and gives a low confidence, which is the right thing to do. Sometimes it's confidently wrong. Press "train again from scratch" a few times and watch the answer change — each run starts from different random weights. The lesson: the calibration bars only hold for headlines like the ones it was tested on. Show a model something unlike anything in its data and its confidence stops meaning much.
  • The "sure enough" slider. Slide it up: the computer handles fewer headlines but makes fewer mistakes. Slide it down: it handles more and gets more wrong. There's no correct setting. A spam filter can afford mistakes; a hospital can't. Choosing the line is a human's job.

What our toy can't do

Our model shares the shape of a System One answer — typed, one pass, with a probability — but it's a long way from Jev or Clef:

  • Its questions are baked in. Ours can only ever answer "which of these four topics?" because that's what it was trained on. The real ones read your question fresh each time, so you can ask about spam today and urgency tomorrow without re-training.
  • It ignores word order. We average the word rows, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical to it. Real models keep the order, using the attention from {{ch:attention}}.
  • It only knows four boxes. Everything in the universe has to be world, sports, business or scitech. It will file your homework excuse under one of them.

None of that changes the big idea. A chatbot and a decision model are the same kind of machine pointed at different jobs: one predicts the next token, again and again; the other predicts the answer, once.
