Every network you have trained so far has had exactly one hidden layer: input → hidden → output. Real networks stack more: input → hidden → hidden → output, and the big ones go dozens of layers deep. The number of layers is called the network's depth. The wiring rule does not change at all. The outputs of hidden layer 1 simply become the inputs of hidden layer 2. Every neuron in layer 2 does the same multiply-add-squish from {{ch:neuron}}; it just works on layer 1's answers instead of on the raw input.

Why would that help? The network in {{ch:hidden}} built its answer out of straight lines, because each hidden neuron looks at the raw inputs, and one neuron's question is always a line. A second hidden layer builds shapes out of those shapes: layer 1 draws lines, and layer 2 bends and combines them into curves and pockets.

Here is a puzzle that makes depth visible: two spirals, tangled around each other. Every dot is a training example. Its position on the graph paper is the input (two numbers, x and y), and its color is the right answer. This is the dots-on-graph-paper picture from {{ch:tables-dots}}, all grown up. If XOR was the puzzle one neuron could not solve, the spiral is XOR's final boss.

You have 8 hidden neurons to spend on the spiral. You can put all 8 in one layer, or split them into two layers of 4. Which arrangement gets further?

Two layers of 4, at least from the starting weights this page uses. Eight straight lines added together are not enough to follow the curl. Four lines fed into four more neurons are. Train both below and compare.

Look at the first two rows. Both networks have 8 hidden neurons and nearly the same number of stored numbers. The one-layer network stalls, and more training barely helps: it is still at 83% after 8,000 epochs. The two-layer network finds the spiral.


The little neuron maps show why. In the one-layer network every map is a stripe, and the output can only add eight stripes together. In the two-layer network, layer 1 makes four stripes and layer 2 turns them into four bent shapes, and bent shapes are what a spiral is made of.



Is deeper always better?


No, and the other two rows show it. Give a single layer 16 neurons and it solves the spiral too. That is a famous result called the universal approximation theorem: one hidden layer can fit almost any shape, as long as it is wide enough. Depth is not the only way to get there. It is a way to get there with fewer neurons.


Luck matters too. Press "new random start" and train again, and the same network can end up somewhere different. To be fair to all four, we ran each one from 60 random starts, for 8,000 epochs each:


    
  
  • 1 layer × 8 never reached 95%. Its best run got to 92%.
    • 
  
  • 2 layers × 4 + 4 reached 95% in 25 of its 60 starts.
    • 
  
  • 1 layer × 16 reached 95% every single time.
    • 
  
  • 2 layers × 8 + 8 reached 95% in 53 of its 60 starts. Most of the others got stuck near a coin flip and stayed there.
    • 



So the honest summary has two halves. Stacking layers lets the same neurons do more. But deep networks are touchier to train, and more of them get stuck. Much of the engineering in modern deep learning (ReLU from {{ch:activations}}, and the tricks you will meet in {{ch:scale}}) exists to make deep networks train reliably.


Then why is every serious network deep? Because the saving grows with the problem. On a small puzzle like this one, going wide costs a few extra neurons. On large problems with layers of structure, such as pictures made of objects made of parts made of edges, a shallow network can need impossibly many neurons to do what a deep one does with a modest number. A two-color spiral is too small to show that. It can only show you the start of it.


Three footnotes for the curious. The hidden activation in this widget is tanh, sigmoid's cousin that outputs −1 to +1 instead of 0 to 1 (that is why the little maps have both blue and red). The training method is Adam from {{ch:descent}}; plain gradient descent fast enough to watch was so jumpy here that the result depended on which browser you used. And the spiral is the classic demo from TensorFlow Playground, where you can stack up to six hidden layers. This book has its own version in {{ch:playground}}.



Each layer builds on the one before it: lines, then shapes made of lines, then shapes made of shapes. That lets the same number of neurons go further. It is not magic: a wide enough single layer can do the same job, and deeper networks are harder to train.

In a network with two hidden layers, what do the neurons in the second hidden layer receive as inputs?

    
    
    
    
Each layer feeds the next. Layer 2 never sees x and y directly; it sees what layer 1 made of them, which is what lets it build shapes out of shapes.

  

  

    
Eight neurons in one layer stalled on the spiral. The same eight neurons as two layers of four solved it. What is the best explanation?

    
    
    
    
Same data, same training, same neuron count. The difference is the arrangement. And "always" is too strong: with 16 neurons, the one-layer network solved the spiral as well.

  

  

    
One hidden layer of 16 neurons solved the spiral without any help from depth. So why are big networks built deep instead of just wide?

    
    
    
    
A single layer can learn curves, as you saw, and deep networks get stuck more often, not less. The case for depth is efficiency: the harder and more layered the problem, the more neurons depth saves.
