One output neuron per answer

Three possible answers means three output neurons: one for fruit, one for chips, one for cookie. Each possible answer is called a class, and each output neuron produces a score for its class. A higher score means "I think it's this one."

Scores can be any numbers, positive or negative, which makes them awkward to read. So the last step is a function called softmax, which turns a list of scores into probabilities that always add up to 1:

softmax_i = e^(z_i) / (e^(z_1) + e^(z_2) + e^(z_3))

Here z₁, z₂, z₃ are the three scores, and e is a famous fixed number, about 2.718. e^z means "e raised to the power z": e^0 = 1, e^2 ≈ 7.4, and e to a negative power is a small positive fraction. Two things matter here: e^z is always positive, and it grows fast as z grows.

So the top of the fraction makes every score positive and stretches the gaps between them. The bottom divides by the total, which is what makes the answers add up to 1.

softmax([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])  →  [0.66, 0.24, 0.10]

Read that as "66% fruit, 24% chips, 10% cookie." The network's pick is whichever is highest.

Keeping score: how surprised was the model?

To train this network we need a single number that says how wrong it was, so training has something to shrink. That number is called the loss. The single neuron in {{ch:training}} used the simplest one, squared error: take truth − prediction, square it, and average over the examples.

A list of probabilities gets a better score. First write the right answer as a list too: [1,0,0] for fruit, [0,1,0] for chips, [0,0,1] for cookie. Exactly one slot is 1 and the rest are 0. That is called one-hot encoding.

Now look at just one number: the probability the model gave to the right answer. If it said 90% for the snack that turned out to be correct, it is barely surprised. If it said 10%, it is shocked. The cross-entropy loss turns that surprise into a number:

loss = −log(probability the model gave to the right answer)

(log here is the natural logarithm, the ln button on a calculator. It is the undo button for e^.) You never need to work it out by hand. What matters is its shape:

probability given to the right answerlossin words
0.900.11barely surprised
0.500.69coin-flip surprised
0.102.30shocked

A probability of exactly 1 costs 0. As the probability heads toward 0 the loss climbs without limit: 0.01 costs 4.61. So being confidently wrong is punished much harder than being unsure. The network's loss is the average over all its training examples.

The scores are [2.0, 1.0, 0.1]. You add 1 to every score, making them [3.0, 2.0, 1.1]. What happens to the probabilities?

Nothing changes. Adding 1 to a score multiplies its e^z by e, and when every term on the top and bottom of the fraction is multiplied by the same number, it cancels. Softmax only cares about the gaps between scores. Check it with the sliders below.

What just happened

After 5,000 rounds of training the right snack gets a probability above 0.99 on every row, so the bars look almost like a plain yes or no. The scores underneath tell the fuller story: the winning score sits several points above the others, and softmax stretches that gap into near-certainty. The exact scores change each time you reload the page, because training starts from different random weights, but the picks do not.

And the decoy? Toggling "hungry" barely moves anything. The network was never told that input was useless. All eight rows demand the same snack whether hungry is on or off, so training pushed the network until that input stopped changing its answer.

This pattern (one output neuron per class, then softmax, trained with cross-entropy) is how a network reads handwritten digits (10 classes; you will train one in {{ch:digits}}) and how a chatbot picks its next word-piece (tens of thousands of classes or more; see {{ch:chatbots}}). Same idea, more output neurons.

A network's three output scores are all exactly the same. What does softmax give?

Softmax only looks at the gaps between scores. No gaps means no preference, so the probability is shared equally, whatever the scores are.

On one example the model gave the right answer a probability of 0.9. On another it gave the right answer 0.1. How do the two losses compare?

0.11 against 2.30, about twenty times bigger. Cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes far more than it rewards confident hits, which pushes the model hardest exactly where it is most wrong.

The classes are [fruit, chips, cookie]. What is the one-hot label for chips?

One slot per class, with a single 1 in the right class's slot. A plain "2" would wrongly suggest chips is somehow "twice" fruit.
