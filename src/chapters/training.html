Let's teach our one neuron to decide "can I have screen time?"

Rule: screen time is allowed if you practiced piano OR you finished your homework. Here are the examples we'll feed it:

piano done?homework done?screen time allowed?
0 (no)0 (no)0 (no)
1 (yes)0 (no)1 (yes)
0 (no)1 (yes)1 (yes)
1 (yes)1 (yes)1 (yes)

The training loop is the same idea every time:

  1. Start with random weights (the neuron has no clue)
  2. Run all 4 examples through the neuron and see what it predicts
  3. For each one, work out how wrong it was
  4. Nudge every weight a tiny bit in whichever direction would have made the answer slightly more right
  5. Repeat thousands of times

Watch one nudge happen, with real numbers

"Nudge every weight in the right direction" sounds like magic until you watch a single nudge happen. So let's do one, slowly, by hand.

Say the neuron just started, with random numbers: weight 1 (piano) = −0.5, weight 2 (homework) = 0.2, bias = −0.1. We show it the example piano = 1, homework = 0, right answer = YES:

total      = (−0.5 × 1) + (0.2 × 0) + (−0.1)  =  −0.6
prediction = sigmoid(−0.6)                    ≈   0.35
truth      = 1
error      = 1 − 0.35                         =  +0.65   (too low!)

The prediction is too low, so we want the total to come out bigger next time. Go through the three numbers one at a time:

  • Weight 1 (piano): piano was ON (1), so this weight took part in the total. Prediction too low → nudge it UP. (It was −0.5, dragging the answer down, which is exactly the wrong job for it. The nudge starts fixing that.)
  • Weight 2 (homework): homework was OFF (0), so this weight got multiplied by zero. It had no effect at all on this prediction, so it gets no nudge. You can't blame a weight that wasn't involved.
  • Bias: the bias is always involved (it gets added every single time, like an input that's stuck at 1). Prediction too low → nudge it UP.

And if the prediction had been too high? Same logic, flipped: every weight whose input was on gets nudged DOWN. The recipe for one weight is:

nudge = small step × error × that weight's input

The error sets the direction (too low → up, too high → down). The input decides who takes part (an input of 0 wipes its weight's nudge out). And the small step keeps each change tiny, so no single example can yank the neuron around.

Honesty note: the fast trainer further down this page and the book's Python scripts multiply in one more factor, the slope of the sigmoid curve at the current output. Slopes are what {{ch:descent}} and {{ch:backprop}} are about. The direction logic is exactly what you just read.

In the widget below, the same neuron starts with the same three numbers. Suppose you pick the example piano = 0, homework = 0 → NO. The neuron says 0.48, which already counts as a NO. What will one nudge on that example do?

The truth is 0 and the neuron said 0.48, so it is still 0.48 too high. Being on the right side of 0.5 is not the same as having no error. Both inputs were 0, so both weights sit this one out, and only the bias gets nudged: from −0.10 to −0.27. Pick that example and check.

What just happened


Did the NO example surprise you? Press "start over" and then count your presses of "nudge once on each example." The NO example begins on the correct side, at 0.475. The first rounds of nudges push it the wrong way, up to 0.635 by the fourth press, before it turns around. It is not back on the correct side until the 16th press, and that is the first moment all four examples have a ✓.


Three of the four examples want a YES, and every one of them pushes the bias up. Only the NO example pushes it down, and at first it loses. But the two weights are growing too. Once they are big enough to produce a YES without help, the YES examples stop leaning on the bias, their pushes fade, and the NO example drags the bias back below zero. Training is a tug-of-war between the examples, and the numbers settle where the pulls balance.

What just happened


After 1,000 steps the predictions read about 0.08, 0.95, 0.95 and 1.00, whatever the random start. (We tried 2,000 different starts. Every one had all four examples right within 100 steps, and after 1,000 steps they agreed to two decimal places.) They never reach exactly 0 or 1, because sigmoid only gets there with an infinitely large total. Keep training and they keep inching closer, with smaller and smaller nudges.


Nobody told the neuron "if piano OR homework is done, then yes." It got there by being slightly less wrong, over and over. Everything from here on is this same loop, made bigger.


Do not expect every network to forget its starting point like this one. In {{ch:hidden}}, different random starts will find different solutions. And if you would like to watch this loop animated on a bigger network, try the second video in 3Blue1Brown's neural networks series.



Training is a loop: predict, measure the error, nudge every number that took part a little in the direction that shrinks the error, repeat. Nobody types the rule in. It ends up stored in the weights and the bias.

The neuron's prediction was too high on an example where piano was 1 and homework was 0. Which numbers get nudged?

    
    
    
    
Too high means push the total down. Only the numbers that took part get moved: weight 1 (its input was on) and the bias (always on). Weight 2 was multiplied by 0, so it had nothing to do with the mistake.

  

  

    
Why is each nudge kept small?

    
    
    
    
Every example pulls the same few numbers in its own direction. Small steps let the pulls balance out. Small steps are slower, not faster, which is why training takes thousands of rounds.

  

  

    
After training, where is the rule "piano OR homework" stored?

    
    
    
    
There is no rule written anywhere and the examples are not consulted when the neuron answers. Three numbers, tuned by training, are the whole rule.
