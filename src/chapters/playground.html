Every network in this book so far came with its settings already chosen. Somebody picked the data, the number of layers and the learning rate, and you pressed train. In this chapter nobody picks anything for you.

Below is a lab bench with every part from Parts 1 and 2 on it: dots on graph paper, neurons, hidden layers, activations, a learning rate, a test set. You choose the dots. You build the network. You press play and find out whether your guess was right.

Nothing in the lab is pre-trained. Each time you press play, a network with random weights starts learning in your browser, using the backpropagation you stepped through in {{ch:backprop}}.

One color code runs through the whole lab: orange means a positive number and blue means a negative one. An orange dot is an example whose right answer is "orange". The network answers "orange" wherever its output neuron's weighted sum comes out positive, and "blue" wherever it comes out negative.

The lab opens with a circle of orange dots inside a ring of blue ones, and a network with one hidden layer of 4 neurons. Once it has learned to separate them, what shape will its orange region be?

Press play and look. Each hidden neuron can only draw one straight line (that was the lesson of {{ch:tables-dots}}), so four neurons can fence the circle in with at most four sides. The tanh squish rounds the corners, but the shape never becomes a true circle.

Reading the lab

  • The big map is the graph paper from {{ch:tables-dots}}. Each dot is one example: its position (two numbers, x and y) is the input, and its color is the right answer. The background shows what the network would answer at every point on the paper.
  • The diagram is the network itself. Each square is one neuron, drawn as a tiny copy of the map that shows where that neuron's output is positive or negative. Squares in the first hidden layer are always split by a straight line. Squares further along are bent, because they are built out of the squares before them. Point at a square to enlarge it, and click it to pin it to the big map.
  • The two scores. The training score is the share of the training dots the network gets right. The test score uses 200 more dots from the same pattern that it never trains on. Tick "show the test dots" to see them.
  • The buttons. One step is one epoch: one pass through all the training dots. Reset goes back to epoch 0 with the same starting weights. New start rolls new random ones.
  • The knobs. The learning rate is the step size from {{ch:descent}}. Batch size is how many dots the network looks at before each nudge of its weights. The shape of the network, meaning how many layers it has and how many neurons are in each, is called its architecture, and the lab shows how many stored numbers that shape needs.

Honesty note, in two parts. First, the noise slider gives some training dots the wrong color and draws a black ring around each one. The test dots always keep their true color, so the test score tells you how well the network knows the real pattern. That is the same choice {{ch:overfitting}} made, and real test sets are not so tidy. Second, the lab learns by plain mini-batch gradient descent, with none of the extras that PyTorch's Adam adds in {{ch:pytorch}}. That makes it slower and fussier than a professional setup, but it means every knob does exactly what its chapter said it does.

Lab notebook

Nine missions, each tied to a chapter you have read. Every one has a button that sets the lab up for you and a box to tick when you have cracked it. Your ticks are remembered on this device: 0 of 9 done.

Under each mission is a note of what we found when we ran it. Try it yourself before you open the note. Our numbers come from the exact setup behind each button, and that setup always starts from the same random weights, so if you press play without changing anything you should see the same numbers. (Different browsers round the last digit of their arithmetic differently, and over a thousand epochs that can move a result by a few epochs.) Press new start and your numbers will differ more. How much that matters is the lesson of mission 7.

  1. The lonely neuron

    Take away every hidden layer and you are left with the single straight-line neuron from {{ch:tables-dots}}. Which of the five sets of dots can it solve, and what do those have that the others lack?

    What we found

    Only the two blobs: 100% within 2 epochs. On the two moons its test score stalled at about 92%. After 300 epochs it was at 43% on the ring, 49% on the XOR corners and 58% on the spirals. Its line keeps swinging about on those three, but it never does reliably better than a coin flip. The blobs are the only set where one straight line can separate the colors. That is what linearly separable means. With no hidden layers the lab draws the neuron's line as a dashed line, so you can watch it swing into place.

  2. XOR on a budget

    {{Ch:hidden}} solved a four-dot XOR with a small hidden layer. This XOR has 200 dots. Starting from one hidden neuron and adding one at a time, what is the smallest hidden layer that gets the test score to 95%?

    What we found

    Three. One neuron scored 67% on the test. Two drew a diagonal stripe and stopped at about 86%: over ten different random starts, two neurons never got past 90%. Three reached 96% by epoch 300, and got to 95% or better in 7 starts out of 10. Five did it every time. A stripe was enough for four dots sitting on the corners. It is not enough when the dots fill the whole corner regions, because the far ends of each region stick out of the stripe.

  3. Three sides

    Fence in the orange circle with exactly three hidden neurons, then take one away and train again. Why is three the smallest number that works? The line-drawing rule from {{ch:tables-dots}} is all you need.

    What we found

    With three neurons the network scored 100% on its training dots by epoch 72, and the orange region is a triangle with rounded corners. With two it stalled at 86% on training and 82% on the test. Each hidden neuron draws one straight line, and you cannot close a fence with fewer than three straight sides. Two lines leave a gap, however you turn them.

  4. Cram, then cure

    This is {{ch:overfitting}} with every control unlocked: the biggest network, only 60 training dots, and 9 of them the wrong color (ringed in black). On fast, run it to epoch 1,500 and watch the two lines on the chart, then cure it at least three different ways.

    What we found

    The test score peaked at 98% at epoch 113. Then the network began bending its boundary around the ringed dots. By epoch 1,500 its training score was 97% and its test score was down to 85%. Four cures, each tried alone from the same setup and scored on the test at epoch 1,500: more data (400 training dots: 94%), a smaller network (one hidden layer of 8: 93%), weight decay, which {{ch:overfitting}} named but did not build (set "discourage big weights" to 0.01: 97%, and you can watch the lines in the diagram stay thin), and stopping early (pause at the peak instead of running on: 98%). Push that last slider to 0.03 and you learn something else: the score drops to 50%, because every weight is being squashed toward zero faster than training can grow it.

  5. Too hot, too cold

    {{Ch:descent}} warned that a learning rate can be too big or too small. On the spirals, find one that makes training fall apart, one that makes it crawl, and the fastest one that still works. Switch the chart to loss to see the damage.

    What we found

    With this setup, 0.1 reached 95% on the training dots at epoch 717, and 0.3 got there sooner, at epoch 423. At 1 the network touched 96% at epoch 330 and then lost it again, bouncing between 49% and 98%. At 3 and at 10 it never learned: at 10 the loss, which starts near 0.7, leapt as high as 33. Going the other way, 0.01 had reached only 58% after 2,000 epochs, and at 0.001 the loss crept from 0.671 to 0.669 in 2,000 epochs. That is still learning, just far too slowly to be any use.

    For a real explosion, press "the exploding one" and then play: ReLU, four layers of 16, a batch size of 1 and a learning rate of 10. In ten tries out of ten the weights shot past a billion inside the first epoch, and the lab stops with a warning. Tanh and sigmoid never did this in any run we made. Their outputs cannot go beyond 1, and that keeps every nudge small. A ReLU output has no ceiling, so big weights make big outputs, big outputs make bigger nudges, and bigger nudges make bigger weights.

  6. Squisher showdown

    Same spirals, same two layers of 8 neurons, three activations from {{ch:activations}}. Which gets the training score to 95% first: tanh, ReLU or sigmoid? Look closely at the edges of the shapes each one draws.

    What we found

    ReLU got there at epoch 469 and tanh at epoch 717. Sigmoid was still at 60% after 2,000 epochs. Over eight random starts of 1,500 epochs each, tanh solved it 8 times, ReLU 7 times and sigmoid never. ReLU's shapes have straight edges and sharp corners. A ReLU neuron's map is perfectly flat on one side of a straight fold and a steady slope on the other, so everything built from such maps is made of flat pieces. Tanh draws smooth curves. Sigmoid is not broken, only slow: its curve is flatter, so each nudge is smaller. With the learning rate turned up to 1 and 3,000 epochs to work with, it solved the spirals in 3 starts out of 6.

  7. Wide against deep

    One hidden layer of 16 neurons has 65 stored numbers, and so do two hidden layers of 8 and 4. Race them on the spirals, the showdown from {{ch:depth}}: which reaches 95% on the training dots first?

    What we found

    From these two setups, the two-layer network reached 95% at epoch 1,097 and the wide one at epoch 2,783. We repeated the race from 12 different random starts. The two-layer network got there first 11 times, in about 1,300 epochs (the middle result of the 12) against about 2,700. One honest surprise: the wide network is slow, not helpless. Given 4,000 epochs it solved the spirals in 11 starts out of 12.

  8. One at a time, or all at once

    The mini-batches of {{ch:descent}} are the batch size slider, and this setup nudges the weights after every single dot. Count the epochs until the ring closes, then press reset, slide batch size to "all" and count again.

    What we found

    With a batch size of 1 the training score passed 95% at epoch 4. With 16 it took 49 epochs, and with all 200 dots in one batch it took 627. An epoch is one pass through the dots either way, but with a batch of 1 that pass contains 200 nudges, and with one big batch it contains exactly one. The price of small batches shows on the chart: each nudge is based on very few dots, so the line wobbles more.

  9. The unlearnable

    Draw a set of dots that even the biggest network (four layers of 16) cannot get above 60% on the test. What is your drawing missing that the spirals have? Think of the memorizing in {{ch:overfitting}}.

    What we found

    Confetti works: orange and blue dots scattered with no pattern at all. With 480 dots the network scored between 64% and 79% on the dots it trained on and between 40% and 56% on the held-back ones, which is a coin flip. With only 80 dots it memorized more than 90% of its training dots and still did no better than a coin flip on the test. The spirals are hard, but they follow a rule, so knowing some dots tells you about the others. Confetti has no rule. There is nothing to learn, only answers to memorize. A checkerboard is the in-between case: with 4 squares along each side the test score reached about 90%, and with 8 it fell to about 70%. (To keep this fair we did not draw by hand: a short program scattered our dots and colored them.)

How to experiment like a scientist

A lab like this makes it easy to click around until something works. That is fun, and it teaches you very little, because afterwards you cannot say why it worked. Four habits turn clicking into finding out.

  1. Change one thing at a time. If you add a layer and raise the learning rate together and the score improves, you do not know which change did it. Changing one thing and holding everything else still is called a controlled experiment. The reset button exists for this: it replays the same starting weights, so the one thing you changed is the only difference.
  2. Guess before you press play. Say it out loud or write it down: "two neurons will get about 90%". A guess that turns out wrong is the most useful thing that can happen to you in a lab, because it shows you exactly which idea in your head needs fixing.
  3. Write down what happened. The settings, the epoch, both scores. Memory rounds everything toward what you expected. The copy link button stores the whole setup in a web address, which makes it a good thing to paste into your notes.
  4. Repeat with a different random start before you believe it. A network begins from random weights, and a computer makes its "random" numbers from a starting number called a random seed. A different seed means a different start, and sometimes a different ending.

Here is how much the fourth habit matters. When we first ran mission 7, the wide network won: it reached 95% at epoch 2,989, ahead of the deep one at epoch 3,281. If we had stopped there, this book would now be telling you that wide beats deep. We ran the race again from eleven more random starts, and the deep network won all eleven. One run is a story. Twelve runs are evidence.

A network's behavior comes from a handful of choices: the data, the shape, the activation, the learning rate. You understand a choice once you have changed it, guessed what would happen, and watched.

Challenge

Build the smallest network you can (fewest stored numbers) that gets the test score on the two spirals to 95%. We managed it with 43 stored numbers, though it needed about 2,000 epochs. When you beat that, press "copy link to this setup" and send the link to a friend with a dare attached. The link rebuilds your exact dots, network and starting weights on their screen.

A network with no hidden layers trains on the two moons. Where does its test score end up?

One neuron draws one straight line. A straight line can cut most of one moon away from the other, but the tips of the moons hook around each other, and no straight line gets those. More epochs do not help, because the limit is the shape, not the training. You can check this with mission 1.

The biggest network is training on 60 noisy dots. Its training score is still climbing, but its test score peaked a while ago and is now falling. Which of these will not help?

A falling test score while the training score climbs means the network has moved on from learning the pattern to memorizing the wrong-colored dots. More epochs give it more time to memorize. More data, a smaller network, weight decay and stopping early all push the other way. Mission 4 lets you try each one.

You race a wide network against a deep one on the spirals, once each, and the wide one wins. What should you do before telling anyone that wide beats deep?

Each run begins from different random weights, and a single run can go either way. That happened to us: the wide network won our first race and lost the next eleven. Changing the learning rate until you get the answer you wanted is not an experiment at all.
