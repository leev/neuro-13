The snack picker had three classes and three tidy yes/no inputs. Real classifiers usually have more classes and messier inputs. So here is the snack picker's big sibling: a small network that guesses which meal you are describing from menu-style clues.

Turning words into inputs

A neural network wants numbers, not words. In {{ch:multi}} you used one-hot encoding for the labels. This time it goes on the inputs too. A word choice like course = dessert becomes three yes/no inputs:

course_main = 0
course_snack = 0
course_dessert = 1

Same for temperature, taste, diet, and base. The network never receives the word "pastry." It receives base_pastry = 1 with the other four base inputs set to 0. Five word choices become 14 inputs in all.

What just happened


With the diet missing, most of the vote goes to the two pasta dishes. How they split it changes from run to run: sometimes close to even, sometimes more like 30/70, and now and then a third meal sneaks in a share. That is worth noticing. The network was only ever trained on complete meals, so nothing in training pinned down what it should say about half a meal. It was left to improvise. Fill in the diet and you are back on ground it was trained on, and one bar takes over.


Inside, the hidden neurons have become detectors for combinations of clues. One might respond to cold + sweet, another to pastry, another to main + pasta. Nobody assigned those jobs. Training nudged the weights until useful patterns appeared, and a different run will hand the jobs out differently. Working out what the hidden neurons in real models have learned is a research field of its own, called interpretability.


Press "show learned numbers" in the widget if you have not yet. This is the grown-up sibling of the dark "model" panels you have been watching since {{ch:training}}. When someone says "I downloaded the weights," a much longer list like this one is what they got.




  How many hidden neurons? That number is chosen by the builder before training starts. Training changes the weights and biases; it never adds neurons. A setting like this, picked by a person rather than learned, is called a hyperparameter. Try 2: the network usually gets stuck below 1.000, because two hidden neurons are too few to tell eight meals apart. Then try 8 or 12, which almost always get there. Bigger is not automatically better, though. A network with lots of spare room can simply memorize its training examples instead of finding the pattern behind them. That is called overfitting, and {{ch:overfitting}} lets you watch it happen.

Why does "base" get five separate yes/no inputs instead of one input numbered 1 to 5?

    
    
    
    
If pastry were 1 and fruit were 5, the network would be told that fruit is "five times pastry" and that potato sits between pastry and pasta. One-hot inputs say only "it is this one, and not the others."

  

  

    
You describe a hot, savory pasta main and leave the diet blank. The network splits its vote between spaghetti and mac & cheese. What does that tell you?

    
    
    
    
A split vote is the honest answer when the evidence fits more than one meal. A model that was 100% sure here would be worse, not better.

  

  

    
Who decides how many hidden neurons the meal guesser has?

    
    
    
    
The number of hidden neurons is a hyperparameter: a choice made by the builder. Training only adjusts the weights and biases inside the shape it was given.
