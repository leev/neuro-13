A neural network is a very large pile of tiny math equations. Together they turn an input (like a picture, or a sentence) into an output (like "this is a cat" or the next word in a story).

Each tiny equation has some adjustable numbers called weights. At the start the weights are random and the network is terrible. You show it lots of examples where you already know the right answer. Each time it gets one wrong, you nudge every weight a tiny bit in the direction that would have made it slightly less wrong. (How does it know which direction is "less wrong"? In {{ch:training}} you'll do one of these nudges yourself, with real numbers, and {{ch:descent}} and {{ch:backprop}} explain how the direction is worked out for millions of weights at once.)

Do this millions or billions of times and the weights end up encoding a surprising amount of knowledge about the world.

That's the main idea. The rest of the book shows how far that idea can stretch.

Is it a brain?

The name is borrowed from the brain. Your brain is a web of cells called neurons that pass tiny signals to each other, and each one "fires" when the signals arriving at it add up to enough. The math version you're about to meet was loosely inspired by that. Loosely is the key word.

A real neuron is a living cell, far more complicated than a line of arithmetic. Your brain has about 86 billion of them, each wired to thousands of others, and they talk in brief electrical spikes whose timing matters. The math version just passes one number along. And nobody has found the brain using the nudging method in this book; how brains learn is still an open question. What's inside ChatGPT is not a simulated brain. It's arithmetic that borrowed a name.

Everything becomes numbers

Arithmetic only works on numbers, so before a network can do anything with a picture, a sentence or a sound, somebody has to turn it into numbers. The rest of the book leans on this, so here are the three you'll meet most.

A picture is a grid of numbers. Zoom far enough into any photo and it turns into tiny squares called pixels. Each pixel is stored as a number that says how bright it is.

That smiley is 64 numbers. A color photo keeps three numbers per pixel (how much red, green and blue), so a 12-megapixel phone photo is 36 million numbers.

Text is a list of id numbers. Your computer already stores every letter as a number: c is 99, a is 97 and t is 116, so "cat" is 99, 97, 116. Language models use a different list, where whole chunks of words get one id each. You'll build one of those lists in {{ch:tokens}}.

Sound is a list of samples. A microphone measures how hard the air is pushing on it, thousands of times a second. Each measurement is one number, called a sample. One second of CD-quality music is 44,100 of them.

So when this book says a network "looks at a photo" or "reads a sentence," it means a list of numbers went in. What comes out is numbers too: 0.93 might mean "93% sure this is a cat."

Where this book is going

  1. One neuron (you are here). The tiny machine everything else is built from: how it decides, how it learns, and the small puzzle it can't solve until it gets help in {{ch:hidden}}.
  2. How networks learn. More answers, more layers and real data, starting at {{ch:multi}}. You'll step through the one algorithm that trains every network ({{ch:backprop}}) and watch a network memorize its homework instead of learning ({{ch:overfitting}}).
  3. Machines that read and write. Turn text into numbers ({{ch:tokens}}), train a model that invents names ({{ch:names}}), and see what a chatbot is really doing ({{ch:chatbots}}).
  4. Seeing, creating, playing. A network that reads your handwriting ({{ch:digits}}), one that turns noise into pictures ({{ch:diffusion}}), and one that learns by trial and error ({{ch:rl}}).
  5. Your turn. The tools professionals use ({{ch:pytorch}}), a playground for breaking things ({{ch:playground}}), and projects of your own ({{ch:projects}}).

Parts 1 to 4 each end with a checkpoint: what to remember, a mixed quiz, and something to build.
